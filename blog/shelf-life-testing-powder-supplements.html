How to Determine Shelf Life for a Powder Supplement

Ole Hovde·2026-09-21·6 min read

Your label will have a best-by date. How do you know what that date should be?

Most new brands answer this question the same way: they pick a number that sounds reasonable — 18 months, 24 months — based on what they've seen on competitor products, and print it on the label. That's not how shelf life works, and it can create serious problems if a retailer pulls your product for freshness testing or if a consumer experiences quality issues before the date you printed.

What shelf life actually means

Shelf life is the period during which a product maintains its intended quality, safety, and efficacy under defined storage conditions. For a powder supplement, that means the product continues to meet label claims, remains free from spoilage, and maintains acceptable taste, texture, and appearance.

Temperature, humidity, light exposure, packaging barrier properties, and the specific chemistry of your formula all interact to degrade your product in ways that are specific to your product — not to your category in general. A 24-month shelf life for a creatine monohydrate sachet in foil laminate and a 24-month shelf life for a probiotic blend in a transparent pouch are completely different claims with completely different testing requirements behind them.

Real-time shelf life testing

The most straightforward approach: produce a batch of product, store it under controlled conditions (typically 25°C and 60% relative humidity, which approximates typical ambient storage), and test samples at defined intervals — 3 months, 6 months, 12 months, 18 months, 24 months. At each interval, you test against your product specification to confirm the product still meets its quality standards.

The obvious limitation: real-time testing takes as long as the shelf life you're trying to prove. If you want to claim 24 months, you need 24 months of data. Most brands can't wait two years before launching, which brings us to accelerated testing.

Accelerated shelf life testing (ASLT)

Accelerated shelf life testing uses elevated temperature and humidity — typically 40°C and 75% relative humidity — to simulate aging at a faster rate. The underlying principle is the Arrhenius equation: roughly speaking, each 10°C increase in temperature doubles the rate of most degradation reactions. Six months at 40°C may therefore approximate 24 months at 25°C.

ASLT lets you estimate shelf life before launch with reasonable confidence, then confirm with real-time data over time. Food and supplement testing laboratories that specialize in stability work can design an ASLT protocol for your specific product and help you interpret the results.

One important caveat: ASLT is an estimate, not a guarantee. Some degradation mechanisms don't follow the simple temperature relationship. Certain vitamins, probiotics, and moisture-sensitive products behave differently than the model predicts. Use ASLT to get to market with a reasonable claim, then run concurrent real-time testing to confirm it.

What actually degrades in powder supplements

Different ingredients fail in different ways. Understanding what's in your formula helps you design a testing protocol that actually monitors the right things.

Vitamins. Fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) and vitamin C are sensitive to oxygen and moisture. Products with significant vitamin content need barrier film that limits oxygen and moisture transmission, and potency testing at each stability interval.

Botanical extracts. Many herbal extracts lose potency, change color, or develop off-flavors over time, especially with heat and moisture exposure.

Probiotics. Highly sensitive to both temperature and moisture. If your product contains live cultures, viable cell count needs to be tested at every stability interval — not just at the end. Most probiotic products claim viability "at time of manufacture," which is a very different claim than "at time of use."

Omega fatty acids. Fish oil, flaxseed, and other omega-3 sources oxidize over time. Oxidized oil smells rancid and can be harmful. Peroxide value testing is standard for these products.

Proteins. Relatively stable but can clump with moisture uptake and develop off-flavors over time, particularly with inadequate barrier film.

The inventory implication

Most retailers won't accept product with less than 6 months of remaining shelf life. Amazon has its own requirements. This means that a product with an 18-month shelf life is effectively unsellable through most retail channels after month 12. If you're sitting on inventory that's past that threshold, you have a problem.

This is another version of the argument for starting small: don't produce more than you can realistically sell in the first half of your product's shelf life. The math on inventory versus shelf life is part of the Real Runs guide.
