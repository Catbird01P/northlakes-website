What Is Vertical Form Fill Seal (VFFS) Packaging, and Why It Matters for Your Brand

Ole Hovde·2026-09-12·5 min read

Vertical form fill seal — VFFS — is the equipment category behind most of the flexible packaging you already know: stick packs, sachets, stand-up pouches. If you're evaluating a copacker's capabilities list and see "VFFS," here's what it actually means for your product.

What VFFS does mechanically

A VFFS machine takes a roll of flat packaging film, forms it into a tube vertically, seals the bottom, drops a measured dose of product in from above, seals the top, and cuts the finished pack free — all in one continuous, automated motion. The "vertical" in the name refers to the direction the film travels through the machine, as opposed to horizontal form-fill-seal (HFFS) equipment, which builds packs lying flat and suits different product shapes.

Why it matters once you scale past hand-fill

Hand-filling — loading premade pouches or bags manually — is genuinely the right call for small batches: no machine minimum, low setup cost, total flexibility on format. But it doesn't scale. A VFFS line changes the economics entirely once you're running real volume, for a few concrete reasons:

  • Speed: VFFS equipment fills, seals, and cuts continuously, running thousands of units per hour rather than dozens per hour by hand.
  • Consistency: every pack gets the same fill weight and seal integrity, which matters for both label accuracy and shelf-life.
  • Format flexibility: the same class of equipment, with different tooling, produces stick packs, sachets, and stand-up pouches — letting a copacker offer multiple formats without an entirely separate production line for each.

What it means for cost at volume

The tradeoff is upfront: VFFS equipment is a real capital investment, and every changeover between products still costs setup time. That's exactly why minimum order quantities exist industry-wide (we cover that in more depth in our MOQ breakdown) — the equipment's speed advantage only pays off once a run is long enough to amortize the setup cost. For a brand doing a few thousand units, hand-fill or a flat-rate small-batch tier is usually the better economic fit. Past that volume, VFFS becomes meaningfully cheaper per unit.

Questions worth asking about a copacker's VFFS capability

  • What fill weight range does their equipment actually handle well? (Very light or very heavy fills can push outside a machine's sweet spot.)
  • How many flavor changeovers can they realistically run in a day, and at what cost?
  • Is the equipment already running, or "on order"? Both are fine to work with — but you should know which, and plan your timeline accordingly.
