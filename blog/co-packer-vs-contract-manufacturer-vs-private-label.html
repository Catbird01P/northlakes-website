Co-Packer vs. Contract Manufacturer vs. Private Label — What's the Actual Difference

Ole Hovde·2026-09-21·5 min read

If you've spent any time researching how to get your product made, you've probably seen the terms co-packer, contract manufacturer, private label, white label, and nutraceutical manufacturer used almost interchangeably — sometimes by the same company on the same page. The industry is genuinely inconsistent about this. Here's what each one actually means.

Co-packer

A co-packer (short for contract packer) manufactures and packages your product under your brand using your formula. You own the recipe. You own the brand. You own the finished product. They own the equipment, the facility, and the labor to produce it at scale.

For a powder product, that means blending your ingredients, filling sachets or pouches on a form-fill-seal machine, sealing, cutting, and delivering finished goods. Some co-packers also handle fulfillment. Most don't formulate — they manufacture what you bring them.

This is what North Lakes does.

Contract manufacturer

Contract manufacturer is often used as a synonym for co-packer, but it can mean something more: a facility that also handles formulation, ingredient sourcing, and sometimes regulatory development. Instead of bringing them your formula, you bring them a concept and they build the product.

Contract manufacturers are more common in pharmaceuticals and regulated supplement categories where the formulation expertise is highly specialized. For most food and supplement brands, the distinction is academic — you're looking for someone to run your formula, not someone to create it for you.

If you need help developing a formula, a contract manufacturer or a food scientist consultant is the right starting point. Once the formula is finalized, a co-packer handles production.

Private label / white label

Private label and white label are fundamentally different from both of the above. In a private label arrangement, the manufacturer already has a product they make — a protein powder, an electrolyte blend, a greens formula — and you license the right to put your brand on it. You don't own the formula. You didn't develop it. You're a marketing company selling someone else's product with your label on it.

Private label is faster and cheaper to launch. The products are already developed, already tested, often already certified. You pick a formula, pick a flavor, design a label, and you're selling within weeks.

The downside: your product is identical to every other brand using the same manufacturer's base formula. On Amazon, this is common enough that sophisticated buyers recognize the same product under fifteen different brands. There's no real product differentiation, and the manufacturer can license the same formula to your direct competitors.

If you want to own your product — the formula, the differentiation, the IP — private label isn't the path. If you want speed to market and lowest possible upfront cost, it might be.

Nutraceutical vs. supplement vs. functional food

These are regulatory and marketing terms more than operational ones. A nutraceutical is a food-derived product sold for health benefits. A dietary supplement is specifically regulated under DSHEA (the Dietary Supplement Health and Education Act). A functional food is a conventional food with added health-promoting ingredients.

For co-packing purposes, what matters is whether your product is regulated as a food or a supplement, because the documentation requirements differ — particularly around identity testing, COA requirements, and label claims. Your co-packer needs to know which category your product falls into before production.

The question that actually matters

When you're evaluating manufacturers, the question isn't which category they fall into — it's whether they can run your specific product format at your volume with the documentation your category requires. A co-packer who handles powder sachets and has supplement cGMP documentation in place is what most early-stage brands need. Everything else is vocabulary.
