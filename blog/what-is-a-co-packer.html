GETTING STARTED

What Is a Co-Packer? A Plain-English Guide for Food & Supplement Brands

Ole Hovde·2026-09-12·6 min read

If you've started asking around about "co-packers," you've probably also run into the terms "contract manufacturer," "private label," and "contract packager" being used almost interchangeably — sometimes by the same person in the same sentence. That's not you missing something. The industry itself isn't consistent about it. Here's the plain-English version.

What a co-packer actually does

A co-packer (short for "contract packer") is a company that manufactures and/or packages your product for you, using your formula or recipe, under your brand. You bring the product idea and the label; they bring the equipment, the facility, and the labor to actually produce it at scale.

For a powder product specifically — a protein blend, an electrolyte mix, a supplement powder — that usually breaks down into a few concrete jobs:

  • Blending: combining raw ingredients to your exact formula, by weight, and checking the batch before it goes anywhere near a packaging line.
  • Filling & packaging: running that blended product into whatever format you've chosen — sachets, stick packs, zipper pouches — at your specified fill weight and count.
  • Kitting & assembly: building multi-packs, retail bundles, or display-ready cases if your product needs more than a single pouch to hit the shelf.
  • Fulfillment (sometimes): picking, packing, and shipping individual orders to your customers, if the same shop offers it.

Co-packer vs. private label vs. contract manufacturer

These three terms get blurred constantly, so here's the actual distinction:

  • Co-packer / contract packager: runs your formula. You own the recipe; they own the equipment and labor.
  • Private label manufacturer: already has a formula (often a "stock" formula shared across multiple brands) and puts your label on it. You're buying their recipe, not bringing your own.
  • Contract manufacturer: a broader term that can mean either of the above, or full product development from scratch — formulation included. Some contract manufacturers also co-pack; not all co-packers do formulation.

If you already have a formula you don't want to hand over or change, you're looking for a co-packer, not a private label shop — even though a lot of directories and search results will lump them together.

Signs you're ready for a co-packer

Most brands make the jump from home or shared-kitchen production to a co-packer for one of a few reasons:

  • You've outgrown what you can physically hand-fill or blend yourself in the time you have.
  • A retail or distribution opportunity needs volume and consistency you can't guarantee in-house.
  • You're spending more time producing product than running the business around it.
  • You need real food-safety practices and documentation that a home kitchen or garage setup can't provide.

The honest complication: most co-packers are built for brands already doing serious volume — minimum orders in the tens of thousands of units are common industry-wide. That's the single biggest reason smaller brands bounce off two or three copackers before finding one that will actually take their order. What to have ready before you call

Whoever you end up talking to, having these ready speeds up the first conversation dramatically: your product type and any allergen or handling notes, target fill weight per unit, expected units and number of flavors, whether you're supplying your own packaging or need it sourced, and your timeline.
