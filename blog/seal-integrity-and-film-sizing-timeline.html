Seal Integrity 101: What to Check, What Goes Wrong, and Why Film Sizing Takes Longer Than You Think

Ole Hovde·2026-09-13·7 min read

A bad seal is the single most common way a finished pouch fails after it's already left the building — and it's almost always preventable with the right checks during production and the right film decisions made weeks before production ever starts.

What actually causes a bad seal

On a horizontal form-fill-seal (HFFS) line, a seal is made with heat, pressure, and dwell time applied through the seal jaws. Every common failure traces back to one of those three being wrong, or to something physically interfering with the seal area:

  • Product in the seal: powder particles caught in the seal area prevent the film from fully fusing, leaving a microscopic channel. This is the single most common cause of a slow leak that doesn't show up until the pouch has been sitting for weeks.
  • Wrong temperature, pressure, or dwell time: too little of any of the three and the seal doesn't fully bond; too much and you can scorch or thin the film at the seal line, weakening it.
  • Film and formulation mismatch: high-fat or oily powders can interfere with a film's sealing layer if the film wasn't chosen with that formulation in mind. Not every film seals the same way against every product.
  • Static-driven product migration: fine powders can pick up static charge during filling and migrate toward the seal area right before it closes — the same root problem as product-in-seal, with a different cause.
  • Worn seal jaws or wrinkled film: mechanical issues that show up gradually rather than all at once, which is why in-line checks matter more than a one-time setup check.

How to actually check for it

Visual inspection catches the obvious failures — wrinkles, visible gaps, product dusted along the seal line. It won't catch a hairline channel leak. That takes a physical test: a burst test (pressurizing the sealed pouch and watching for failure at a known pressure) or a dye penetration test (running a dye solution along the seal line and checking for bleed-through) are both standard, low-cost ways to catch what the eye misses. Ask any co-packer what their in-process seal checks actually are, and how often they run them during a production day — not just at setup.

What we check: fill weight and seal integrity get verified at regular intervals throughout every production run, not just at the start — because a line that was sealing correctly at 9am can drift by 2pm if a parameter shifts.

Why film sizing takes longer than most brands plan for

Here's the part that catches first-time brands off guard: your roll stock film isn't a shelf item. It's custom-printed to your exact pouch dimensions and your specific machine, which means it has to go through its own production cycle before it ever reaches a co-packer's facility.

The realistic sequence: die lines for your pouch size go to your film supplier, your designer builds artwork against those die lines, the film supplier reviews and approves that artwork (checking bleed, safety zones, barcode placement), and only then does the film go into production — typically 10–12 business days from approved artwork, plus transit time. Artwork creation and approval itself commonly takes another one to two weeks, especially on the first round if revisions are needed.

Add it up and a brand starting the film process from zero is realistically looking at six to eight weeks before film is in hand — well before a production date can even be scheduled. Our own standard production turnaround is 30 days from a signed quote to finished product, but that clock assumes your film, ingredients, and COAs are already on-site. Film lead time runs in parallel to that, not inside it, which is exactly why it needs to start early.

Planning backwards from your target date

The reliable way to plan a first run is to work backward from when you actually need finished goods:

  • Start from your target finished-goods date.
  • Subtract shipping time and production/packout — a few days.
  • Subtract our scheduling and production window once materials are on-site.
  • Subtract film transit time.
  • Subtract film production time (10–12 business days from approved artwork).
  • Subtract artwork creation and approval (1–2 weeks).

Run that math and most first-time brands find their film needs to be ordered roughly six to eight weeks before they want product in hand — not two weeks, which is the assumption that causes the most frustration on a first run.

Why the front-loaded cost saves money later

Paying for a proper film-sizing process up front — correct die lines, a real approval round, film matched to your actual formulation — costs time and a bit of design back-and-forth before anything gets produced. Skipping it to save two weeks is how a brand ends up with a full production run of pouches with a seal defect that only shows up after they've shipped to customers: reprint costs, a recall conversation, and a damaged relationship with whoever's shelf that product was headed to. The added cost on the front end is real. It's a rounding error next to what a seal failure costs on the back end.
