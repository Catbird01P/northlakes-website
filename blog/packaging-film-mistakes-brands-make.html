PACKAGING

The Packaging Film Mistakes That Burn First-Time Brands

Ole Hovde·2026-09-21·7 min read

If there is one single thing that causes more first-run co-packing disasters than anything else, it's film. Brands order it wrong, order it without talking to their co-packer first, order too little of it, or order it from a supplier whose specs don't match the machine that needs to run it. The result is always the same: thousands of dollars of unusable packaging and a delayed production run.

Here's what to know before you order anything.

What roll stock film actually is

Roll stock film is the raw material your sachets or stick packs are made from. It comes on a roll — like a very wide, very fancy roll of tape — and feeds through your co-packer's form-fill-seal machine, which forms the pouch shape, fills it with your product, seals it, and cuts it free. The film is custom-printed with your artwork and specific to your product and machine configuration.

This is not a shelf item. You can't order it from a supplier on Monday and have it by Thursday. Custom-printed roll stock film typically takes 10–12 business days from approved artwork, plus transit time. Factor in design, revisions, and approval and you're realistically looking at 10–12 weeks from concept to film on the floor. Start this process earlier than you think you need to.

The specs you must confirm with your co-packer before ordering

Every form-fill-seal machine has specific requirements. Film that works on one machine won't necessarily work on another. These are the specs you need in writing from your co-packer before you talk to a film supplier:

Core width. The cardboard tube the film is wound around is typically either 3-inch or 6-inch diameter. If your supplier winds on a 6-inch core and your co-packer's machine requires 3-inch, the roll won't physically load. Ask. Get it in writing. Give it to your supplier before they start production.

Maximum roll diameter. There's a physical limit to how large a roll the machine can accept. Your supplier needs to know this number.

Unwind direction. When film feeds off the roll and into the machine, it unwinds in a specific direction. The eye marks — the registration marks the machine's sensors use to align the artwork — need to be on the correct side for the unwind direction your machine requires. If they're on the wrong side, your artwork will be misaligned or your pouches will be oriented wrong. Get the unwind direction from your co-packer in writing and give it to your film supplier before they print anything.

Eye mark specifications. Small, usually black registration marks that the machine detects to align each pouch. Your supplier handles placement, but they need your machine's specification to do it correctly.

Barrier properties — not all film protects the same

The film that looks right for your product isn't always the film that performs right. Barrier properties — specifically moisture vapor transmission rate (MVTR) and oxygen transmission rate (OTR) — determine how well your packaging protects the product inside from the environment outside.

Foil laminate provides the highest barrier and is appropriate for hygroscopic powders, botanical extracts, probiotics, and fat-soluble vitamins. Metalized film (the silver/shiny look most sachets have) provides good moisture protection at lower cost and is appropriate for most protein powders and general wellness blends. Transparent high-barrier film works for non-hygroscopic products where visual shelf presence matters.

The wrong barrier choice can shorten your shelf life dramatically or cause powder clumping inside the pouch. Ask your co-packer what they typically recommend for your product type, and confirm your choice against your shelf life testing.

Digital printing vs. traditional printing

Traditional flexographic printing runs at nearly 1,000 feet per minute. If you're running 50,000 impressions at 4 inches wide, that's only 16,000 feet of film — the setup time is hours, the run time is minutes, and you pay for both. At small volumes, traditional printing is expensive per impression and locks you into a run size.

Digital printing has no plates, lower minimum order quantities, shorter lead times, and the flexibility to make changes between runs without scrapping expensive plates. For a brand running its first few production runs, digital printing is almost always the right choice. Don't commit to traditional printing until you're running hundreds of thousands of impressions and the economics actually make sense.

Order more than you think you need

Film yield loss is real. The form-fill-seal machine uses 200–500 impressions of film to calibrate during startup on each run. Don't order 4,000 impressions expecting 4,000 finished sachets. Order 4,500 and budget the startup waste into your plan. The guide covers the full yield loss picture in detail.
